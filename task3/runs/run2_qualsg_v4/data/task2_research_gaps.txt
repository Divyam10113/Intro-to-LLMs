1. Introduction and Methodology
This submission presents a systematic extraction of research limitations from the base paper, “Adapting
Visual-Language Models for Generalizable Anomaly Detection in Medical Images” (hereafter, MVFA-AD),
together with a novel research problem formulated as a direct continuation of that work. The limitations
were not identified manually; rather, they were generated through a multi-agent large language model
(LLM) pipeline, following the methodology proposed in “Multi-Agent LLMs for Generating Research
Limitations” (arXiv:2601.11578). This approach decomposes the task of critical appraisal — which is
traditionally performed holistically and subjectively by a human reviewer — into a modular sequence of
specialized agent roles, each responsible for a narrow analytical function, so that the final synthesis
reflects converging evidence from complementary perspectives rather than a single undifferentiated pass
over the text.

The pipeline was implemented as a Python orchestration script coordinating five sequential agents, each
instantiated as a distinct prompted role against a large-context language model accessed via an OpenAI-
compatible API client:

   (i) Extractor Agent — performs a literal scan of the base paper for limitations, constraints, and
   future-work statements that are explicitly acknowledged by the original authors.

   (ii) Analyzer Agent — critically inspects the methodology and experimental-setup sections to
   surface implicit limitations, including unrealistic mathematical assumptions, dataset biases, and
   architectural weaknesses not acknowledged by the authors.

   (iii) Reviewer Agent — cross-references the base paper against a broader literature survey to
   identify points at which the paper falls short of the current state of the art (e.g., binary versus fine-
   grained multi-categorical anomaly classification).

   (iv) Judge (Master) Agent — synthesizes the outputs of the three preceding agents, deduplicates
   overlapping findings, resolves contradictions, and drafts an initial, cohesive list of gaps together with
   a preliminary research-problem statement.

   (v) Self-Feedback Agent — performs a final critical pass over the Judge Agent’s output, refining
   terminology and ensuring the language is academically rigorous, objective, and technically precise.

2. Research Gaps and Limitations Identified in the Base Paper
The following limitations were consolidated by the multi-agent pipeline from explicit authorial
statements, methodological analysis, and comparison against the broader literature. They are organized,
as generated, from reporting-level omissions through protocol design, modeling, evaluation, and clinical-
readiness concerns.

  • Limited explicit limitation and future-work reporting. MVFA-AD lacks a dedicated limitations
    section; its only explicit scope caveat is that in-domain evaluation is treated as secondary, and its
    future-work statements are generic. The remaining limitations discussed here were therefore
    inferred from the protocol, architecture, and evaluation design rather than drawn from authorial
    disclosure.

  • The zero-shot protocol is not modality- or anatomy-disjoint. The BMAD leave-one-out setting
    includes two retinal OCT datasets, RESC and OCT17; when one is held out, the other remains in the
    training set. For these pairs, evaluation does not test an unseen medical modality or anatomy, which
    may inflate the reported OCT results. A valid zero-shot protocol should instead group and hold out
    by modality, anatomy, acquisition device, and site simultaneously.

  • Annotation requirements remain clinically heavy. The adaptation objective is trained on pixel-level
    anomaly-segmentation masks using a combination of Dice, Focal, and binary cross-entropy losses
    across four feature levels, including under zero-shot evaluation. This effectively requires dense
    expert annotation for every training image; the method does not formulate an image-level-only,
    sparse, or normal-only supervision variant, which weakens its relevance to label-scarce clinical
    settings.

  • The few-shot setting is underspecified and operationally costly. The K annotated target images
    must simultaneously supply normal exemplars for the memory bank and abnormal examples for
    adapter training, yet the partition between the two is left unspecified; for K = 2 this may be infeasible
    if sufficient exemplars of both classes are required. Clinical few-shot supervision, in practice, may
    contain only normal or entirely unlabeled data. The method further implies per-task retraining, yet
    training time and computational cost are not reported.

• Prompting is static, generic, and domain-agnostic. The text encoder is kept frozen, and the prompts
  consist of hand-crafted normal/abnormal state descriptions averaged over 35 ImageNet templates.
  This design presumes a single, universal visual-language concept of “normal” and “abnormal” that
  holds uniformly across imaging modalities, and it omits instance-adaptive, visually conditioned, or
  hybrid prompting strategies. No prompt-sensitivity analysis is provided.

• Anomaly modeling is binary and lacks clinical granularity. All anomalies are collapsed into a single
  “abnormal” class. The model performs no differentiation of benign-versus-malignant status,
  anomaly subcategory, severity, radiological sign, or differential cost, despite clinical workflows
  requiring category-specific actionability and support for multi-anomaly detection within a single
  case.

• Normal/anomaly disentanglement and distributional asymmetry are not made explicit. The dual-
  adapter design separates anomaly-classification and anomaly-segmentation features, but it does
  not explicitly model the compact distribution of normal samples against the comparatively diverse
  distribution of abnormal samples. The method relies on shared adapted features and a few-shot
  normal memory bank without routing mechanisms, dual normal/abnormal pathways, or many-to-
  one, SigLIP-style regularization.

• Architecture, fusion, and hyperparameters are under-ablated and partly unclear. Numerous
  design choices — including γ = 0.1, λ = 1.0, four feature levels, an input resolution of 240, a learning
  rate of 10−3, a batch size of 16, 50 training epochs, β = 0.5, and fixed prompt templates — are
  presented without accompanying sensitivity analysis. The adapter and projector equations and their
  class-token handling are dimensionally unclear, and the partitioning of the 24-layer Vision
  Transformer into four stages is non-standard, which hinders reproducibility. The final score fusion
  combines zero-shot softmax probabilities on the interval [0, 1] with few-shot cosine distances on
  the interval [0, 2] using a fixed weight of 0.5, without normalization or calibration, so that one branch
  may arbitrarily dominate the fused score.

• Classification and aggregation are outlier-sensitive. Image-level anomaly classification is obtained
  via max-aggregation over pixel-level anomaly scores, so that a single spurious high-scoring pixel is
  sufficient to produce a false positive. No robust aggregation, calibration, or uncertainty-estimation
  mechanism is employed to mitigate this sensitivity.

• Evaluation lacks patient-level leakage controls and variance estimates. Patient-level data splits are
  not reported for either the zero-shot or the few-shot evaluation protocol; the K-shot support images
  may therefore share patients with the held-out test set, artificially inflating measured performance.
  Reported results further appear to reflect single runs, without seeds, confidence intervals, or
  standard deviations — a concern that is especially acute for few-shot sample selection, which is
  known to be highly variance-sensitive.

• Metrics are too narrow for clinical readiness. Only image-level and pixel-level AUC are reported.
  The paper does not report Dice, IoU, or PRO scores; thresholded accuracy, F1, sensitivity, specificity,
  PPV, or NPV; false positives per patient; calibration or expected calibration error (ECE); or

     prevalence-adjusted and rare-category performance. AUC alone can obscure poor performance
     under severe class imbalance and does not by itself demonstrate clinical utility.

  • Baselines and benchmark details are incomplete. Missing comparisons include PatchCore under a
    few-shot normal-only setting, full CLIP fine-tuning, and other vision-language-model adaptation
    methods. In-domain MVTec is evaluated only under the few-shot regime, with zero-shot in-domain
    evaluation absent entirely. The benchmark is described only as “based on BMAD,” without
    specifying official splits, which limits comparability across future work.

  • Scalability, memory, and privacy considerations are unaddressed. The method uses a CLIP ViT-L/14
    backbone and trains six separate leave-one-out models for 50 epochs each on a single RTX 3090
    GPU; training and inference time, memory footprint, and parameter counts are not reported. The
    term “lightweight” refers only to the adapters, not to the overall system. The few-shot memory bank
    grows with K and with spatial resolution, yet retrieval cost and the privacy and governance
    implications of storing patient-derived features are not analyzed. Downscaling to a 240 × 240
    resolution and processing volumetric data as 2D slices may cause small lesions and broader 3D
    CT/MRI context to be lost.

  • Cross-domain, acquisition, and modality generalism is limited. Evaluation covers brain MRI, liver
    CT, retinal OCT, chest X-ray, and histopathology; ultrasound, PET, mammography, and dermoscopy
    are not modeled, nor are shifts in acquisition device, site, or imaging protocol. The medical-specific,
    text-prompt-dependent design may generalize poorly to broader semantic or medical anomaly
    types and to combined industrial-and-medical generalist settings.

  • Interpretability and clinical validation are absent. The model outputs anomaly scores and maps
    only, without language-based reasoning, radiological explanation, or multimodal-LLM-style
    justification. The paper provides no external or multi-institution validation, no prospective or reader
    study, no demographic (sex, age), scanner, or site fairness analysis, no uncertainty or failure-mode
    analysis, and no discussion of regulatory or clinical-workflow integration or independent clinical-
    precision assessment; prior evaluations in the literature suggest that CLIP-based zero-shot anomaly
    detection precision may fall short of clinical requirements.


3. Newly Identified Research Problem
Building directly on the limitations enumerated above, we define the continuation problem of
annotation-light, modality-, site-, and acquisition-disentangled, fine-grained universal medical anomaly
detection under patient-aware distribution shift. Formally, let a multimodal corpus be given as D = {(xi,
mi, ai, qi, pi, yiweak, Sisparse)}, where mi denotes imaging modality, ai denotes anatomy, qi denotes
acquisition, site, and device metadata, pi denotes patient identifier and associated metadata, yiweak
optionally provides image-level or normal-only supervision, and Sisparse is absent or extremely sparse
pixel-level annotation.

The objective is to learn a parameter-efficient foundation-model adaptation fθ that, for an unseen test
patient pt whose modality mt ∉ Mtrain, anatomy at ∉ Atrain, or acquisition/site qt ∉ Qtrain was not observed

during training, jointly predicts: a calibrated anomaly probability s(xt) ∈ [0, 1]; a pixel- or voxel-wise
anomaly map Ŝt; a fine-grained anomaly category or radiological sign ĉt with an explicit abstention option;
and an uncertainty estimate ut. This joint prediction must be produced while enforcing grouped, leakage-
free data splits at the patient, modality, anatomy, site, and acquisition levels simultaneously, and while
avoiding the storage of raw patient-derived features in any retrieval-based memory component.

To be a genuine advance over MVFA-AD rather than an incremental variant, the learning objective must
jointly couple four components: (a) visual-conditioned, instance-adaptive prompt synthesis together with
prototype learning over a compact normal manifold, replacing the fixed, template-averaged prompts of
the base method; (b) an explicit normal–anomaly disentangled latent factorization that models the
asymmetry between a compact normal distribution and a diverse abnormal distribution, and that routes
representations among normal, abnormal, and uncertainty pathways; (c) synthetic or weakly supervised
anomaly generation combined with in-context residual learning driven only by normal or image-level data,
so that dense pixel-level masks are no longer a prerequisite for adaptation; and (d) multi-scale, volumetric
(3D) and small-lesion-preserving adaptation that avoids the information loss introduced by uniform
downscaling and 2D slicing.

Finally, evaluation of any method addressing this problem must use patient-, modality-, anatomy-, site-,
and acquisition-grouped splits, and must report thresholded, clinically meaningful metrics — Dice, IoU,
and PRO for segmentation quality; sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV, and F1 for classification quality;
false positives per patient; calibration error (ECE); and per-category, prevalence-adjusted performance —
together with variance across multiple random seeds and, ideally, external multi-site validation. This
evaluation regime directly addresses the narrow, single-run, AUC-only reporting identified as a limitation
of the base paper, and operationalizes clinical readiness rather than assuming it follows from strong
benchmark AUC alone.
