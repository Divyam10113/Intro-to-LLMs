# Generalizable Vision-Language Models for Zero-Shot / Few-Shot Anomaly Detection in Medical Images: A Literature Review

## 1. Introduction and Motivation

Anomaly detection (AD) in medical imaging — the identification of abnormal or pathological regions without exhaustive supervision — is constrained by a structural feature of the medical domain: annotated abnormal data is scarce, expensive to obtain, and fragmented across institutions due to privacy regulation and data silos [1, 2]. This has motivated a rapid pivot away from fully-supervised, per-disease detectors toward paradigms that require little or no target-domain training data: zero-shot anomaly detection (ZSAD), where no samples from the target dataset are seen at all, and few-shot anomaly detection (FSAD), where only a handful of (typically normal) images are available. The common enabling technology across nearly all recent work is the large-scale pre-trained vision-language model (VLM), and specifically CLIP, whose joint image-text embedding space is repurposed to encode the abstract, often domain-agnostic, notions of "normal" and "abnormal" [2, 3, 4]. The reviewed corpus traces a coherent research trajectory: from foundational demonstrations that CLIP's semantics transfer poorly to medical abnormality detection out-of-the-box [3, 9], through a proliferation of adaptation strategies — prompt learning, lightweight adapters, and hybrid architectures — to more recent efforts toward fine-grained, multi-category, and reasoning-capable detection [1, 8, 10].

## 2. The Core Problem: Semantic Gap Between Class Recognition and Abnormality Recognition

A recurring diagnosis across this literature is that VLMs pretrained on natural image-text pairs are optimized to recognize *object semantics* (what is depicted) rather than *normality/abnormality* (whether what is depicted is anomalous) [3, 12]. AnomalyCLIP [3] articulates this most explicitly: CLIP's zero-shot performance on anomaly detection is weak because its text-image alignment is dominated by foreground-object class semantics, which vary enormously between domains (e.g., industrial defects on manufactured parts versus tumors on organs), whereas the *concept* of abnormality is comparatively domain-invariant. This insight — that normality and abnormality are more generalizable constructs than object identity — becomes the load-bearing assumption for almost every subsequent method in the corpus, whether realized through prompt design [3, 12, 13, 14] or through feature-space adaptation [4, 5, 6, 7].

Compounding this semantic mismatch, medical images introduce an additional domain shift relative to the natural-image and even industrial-inspection distributions on which most VLMs and prior AD methods were benchmarked: divergent imaging modalities (X-ray, ultrasound, MRI, dermoscopy, etc.), highly variable acquisition parameters, and anatomically constrained but visually subtle abnormalities [4, 8]. Huang et al. [4] quantify this explicitly, showing that models transferred naively from natural/industrial ZSAD settings underperform substantially on medical benchmarks, motivating multi-level, pixel-wise alignment losses to "recalibrate" CLIP's visual attention from object semantics toward anomaly cues.

## 3. Prompt-Learning Approaches: Making Text Prompts Object-Agnostic

One major methodological family addresses the semantic gap purely through the *text* branch of CLIP, learning prompts that describe generic (ab)normality rather than specific object classes.

- **AnomalyCLIP** [3] is the foundational instance of this strategy, learning object-agnostic text prompts that capture generic normality/abnormality cues transferable across domains, validated across 17 datasets spanning industrial and medical imaging.
- **GlocalCLIP** [12] extends this idea by explicitly decoupling *global* and *local* prompts, jointly optimized with deep-text prompt tuning and V-V attention in the vision encoder to capture fine-grained local anomaly evidence, complemented by a glocal contrastive objective to align the two prompt levels.
- **AdaCLIP** [13] introduces a *hybrid* prompting scheme combining static prompts (shared across all images, providing a coarse domain-general adaptation) with dynamic, per-image prompts, arguing that neither purely static nor purely instance-conditioned prompting alone suffices for cross-domain generalization.
- **CoPS** [14] pushes prompt conditioning further by synthesizing prompts directly from visual features — extracting normal/anomaly prototypes from patch-level features and injecting them into prompts, while using a variational autoencoder to implicitly fuse sparse class-label information into the prompt space, explicitly targeting the problem that static learnable tokens and overly sparse textual labels limit generalization to unseen categories.

Collectively, this thread shows a progression from *static* object-agnostic prompting [3] to *increasingly instance-adaptive* prompting [12, 13, 14], reflecting a growing recognition that a single fixed textual description of "anomaly" cannot capture the heterogeneity of abnormal appearance across both domains and individual images.

## 4. Adapter-Based Visual Feature Adaptation

A second, largely complementary, family of methods keeps CLIP's backbone frozen (or near-frozen) and inserts lightweight learnable adapters into the visual encoder, arguing that the visual representation itself — not just the text prompt — must be recalibrated toward anomaly-relevant cues.

- **MediCLIP** [2] adapts CLIP for the few-shot medical setting via self-supervised fine-tuning, notably using *synthetic anomaly generation* — simulating disease-like patterns on top of only normal images — to sidestep the absence of labeled abnormal training data entirely.
- **MVFA-AD** (Huang et al.) [4] integrates multiple residual adapters at different depths of the visual encoder, guided by multi-level, pixel-wise vision-language alignment losses, enabling both zero-shot and few-shot operation with reported AUC gains of several percentage points over prior state of the art.
- **AF-CLIP** [5] focuses the adapter explicitly on *local* defect patterns via a multi-scale spatial aggregation mechanism before the adapter, optimizing class-level and patch-level features jointly for classification and localization respectively, then extending to few-shot via memory banks.
- **MadCLIP** [6] proposes a *dual-branch* adapter design that separately models normal and abnormal features, combined with learnable text prompts and, notably, a SigLIP-style loss to handle the many-to-one image-to-unpaired-text relationship — the first reported use of SigLIP loss in the medical AD context — while explicitly avoiding reliance on synthetic data or memory banks, distinguishing it from MediCLIP [2] and AF-CLIP [5].
- **AVA-DINO** [7] generalizes the dual-branch principle further, motivated by the observation that normal and anomalous data are *fundamentally asymmetric* in their distributions (compact vs. diverse), and proposes specialized branches with a text-guided routing mechanism operating over frozen DINOv3 features rather than CLIP, with explicit routing regularization to prevent degenerate uniform branch activation.

The trajectory here moves from adapting *shared* features for all anomaly types [2, 4] toward architecturally separating normal- and anomaly-specific representational pathways [6, 7], suggesting an emerging consensus that a single adapted feature space struggles to represent the asymmetric statistics of compact normal versus diverse abnormal distributions.

## 5. Beyond Binary Detection: Fine-Grained and Multi-Category Anomaly Recognition

A key limitation identified across several papers is that most few-shot/zero-shot AD methods still treat the problem as *binary* one-class classification (normal vs. abnormal), which is clinically insufficient since different anomaly categories (e.g., benign vs. malignant lesions) demand different downstream actions [1, 8].

- **SD-MAD** [1] is explicitly designed for *multi-anomaly* detection, using an LLM to generate diverse textual descriptions of radiological "signs" per anomaly category, under the hypothesis that distinct anomalies within a category share common radiological signs. Its two-stage design first aligns signs with categories by amplifying inter-anomaly discrepancy, then applies an automatic sign-selection strategy at inference to counteract under-fitting caused by limited data — additionally proposing new evaluation protocols specifically for multi-anomaly detection, reflecting the observation that this setting lacks a standardized benchmark.
- **UltraAD** [8] targets fine-grained classification (e.g., benign vs. malignant) in ultrasound imaging specifically, fusing image-level tokens of few-shot visual prototypes with learnable text embeddings, then integrating this with patch-level tokens for localization, and constructing a memory bank of few-shot image-text pairs (with frozen text embeddings but adapted image features) for classification — addressing ultrasound's particular sensitivity to acquisition-device domain gaps.
- **MadCLIP** [6], while primarily an adapter method, is also validated across multiple modalities for both anomaly classification and segmentation, underscoring the field's shift toward multi-task, multi-modality evaluation protocols rather than single-dataset binary benchmarks.

This theme reveals a maturation of the field from proof-of-concept binary AD toward clinically actionable, categorically differentiated detection — a necessary step for real-world deployment, but one that reintroduces some of the label-scarcity challenges (per-category descriptions, per-category prototypes) that ZSAD/FSAD was originally designed to avoid [1].

## 6. Generalist and Cross-Domain Architectures

Rather than specializing for medical imaging, a subset of work aims to train a single detector that generalizes across industrial *and* medical domains simultaneously, treating medical AD as one instance of a broader "generalist anomaly detection" (GAD) problem.

- **InCTRL** [11] formalizes GAD and proposes in-context residual learning: rather than relying on handcrafted text prompts (which the authors argue generalize poorly to medical or semantic anomalies), the model discriminates anomalies by evaluating the residual between a query image and a small set of few-shot *normal* image prompts, exploiting the general principle that anomalies should show larger deviation from normal exemplars regardless of domain. This sidesteps text-prompt design altogether, an implicit critique of the prompt-learning family in Section 3.
- **AVA-DINO** [7] and **GlocalCLIP** [12] similarly report benchmarking across nine and fifteen datasets respectively (spanning industrial and medical domains), and **AdaCLIP** [13] and **CoPS** [14] evaluate on 14 and 13 datasets respectively — establishing that broad cross-domain benchmarking (rather than medical-only evaluation) has become a de facto standard for claiming "generalizability," even for methods whose primary motivation is medical AD.

This cross-domain evaluation convention is itself informative: it suggests the field treats medical imaging not as a wholly separate problem but as one particularly challenging domain-shift regime within a broader generalizable-AD research program, and that success on medical benchmarks is viewed as a stress test of a method's domain-agnostic design.

## 7. Multimodal LLMs and the Move Toward Anomaly *Reasoning*

A distinct and more recent thread moves beyond localization/classification scores toward natural-language *reasoning* about anomalies, leveraging multimodal large language models (MLLMs) rather than CLIP-style dual-encoder architectures.

- **Anomaly-OV** [10] identifies that even strong generalist MLLMs (e.g., GPT-4o) fail to accurately detect and describe fine-grained anomalous details, motivating the first visual-instruction-tuning dataset for AD reasoning (Anomaly-Instruct-125k) and corresponding benchmark (VisA-D&R). Architecturally, its Look-Twice Feature Matching mechanism mimics human visual-inspection behavior by adaptively re-weighting abnormal visual tokens. Although its primary evaluation is general-purpose ZSAD and reasoning, the authors explicitly note extensions to medical and 3D anomaly detection as directions for future work, signaling that reasoning-capable AD for medical images remains comparatively underexplored relative to the adapter- and prompt-based methods above.

This reflects a broader shift in the field's ambition: from producing an anomaly *score* or *segmentation map* to producing a clinically interpretable *explanation*, a capability gap that dual-encoder CLIP-style methods [2–9, 11–14] are not architecturally designed to close.

## 8. Critical Perspectives: Are CLIP-Based Methods Actually Ready for Clinical Use?

Not all work in the corpus is optimistic about the maturity of VLM-based medical AD. Marzullo and Ranzini [9] provide an important counterpoint by directly evaluating CLIP-based ZSAD models — originally developed for industrial inspection — on brain tumor detection using the BraTS-MET dataset. Their finding is that, while these models can transfer *some* general knowledge to medical tasks, their precision falls short of clinical requirements, and they explicitly call for further domain adaptation before such models can be reliably deployed. This paper functions as an empirical check against the more uniformly positive results reported in method-proposing papers [1–8, 10–14], and it substantiates the underlying motivation cited throughout this literature (e.g., in AnomalyCLIP [3] and MVFA-AD [4]) for why naive transfer of industrial ZSAD techniques to medical imaging is insufficient.

## 9. Cross-Cutting Challenges and Open Problems

Synthesizing across the reviewed papers, several structural challenges recur:

1. **Data scarcity as the root motivation.** Nearly every paper cites privacy-driven data silos and expensive expert annotation as the primary justification for the zero-/few-shot paradigm itself [1, 2], rather than as an afterthought.
2. **Object semantics vs. abnormality semantics.** The central technical obstacle — CLIP's bias toward class recognition over anomaly recognition — is addressed through prompt design [3, 12, 13, 14], feature adaptation [2, 4, 5, 6, 7], or exemplar-based residual reasoning [11], but no single solution has been shown to dominate across all evaluation settings.
3. **Normal/anomaly distributional asymmetry.** Compact, low-variance normal distributions versus diverse, high-variance anomaly distributions is treated as a design principle in dual-branch architectures [6, 7], suggesting this asymmetry is increasingly recognized as fundamental rather than incidental.
4. **From binary detection to fine-grained, multi-category recognition.** The shift toward clinically meaningful categorical distinctions [1, 8] reintroduces some of the annotation burden the field originally sought to escape, requiring either LLM-generated category descriptions [1] or curated few-shot category exemplars [8].
5. **Modality and acquisition heterogeneity.** Explicitly flagged in ultrasound [8] and more generally in cross-modality medical benchmarks [4], acquisition-device and protocol variation constitutes a domain-shift axis largely orthogonal to, but compounding, the natural-to-medical domain gap.
6. **Evaluation methodology and standardization.** New protocols proposed specifically for multi-anomaly settings [1], alongside widely varying benchmark suites (9–17 datasets) across papers [3, 7, 11, 12, 13, 14], indicate the absence of a single agreed-upon medical ZSAD/FSAD benchmark, complicating direct cross-paper comparison.
7. **The reasoning gap.** Detection/localization has advanced considerably, but interpretable, language-based reasoning about *why* a region is anomalous remains nascent in the medical setting specifically, with [10] flagging this as future work rather than a solved problem.
8. **Empirical skepticism.** Independent evaluation [9] suggests that even state-of-the-art CLIP-based ZSAD, when applied to a clinically realistic task (brain tumor detection), does not yet meet the precision bar required for clinical deployment — a caution that tempers the generally positive self-reported results elsewhere in this literature.

## 10. Conclusion

The reviewed corpus depicts a fast-moving but still-maturing research area. The dominant strategy — repurposing frozen or lightly-adapted CLIP-style VLMs via prompt learning [3, 12, 13, 14] and/or visual adapters [2, 4, 5, 6, 7] — has produced consistent, incremental gains on cross-domain ZSAD/FSAD benchmarks that include medical imaging as one evaluation axis among several. More medically-specialized efforts are beginning to address clinically meaningful sub-problems: multi-category and fine-grained anomaly differentiation [1, 8], generalist architectures explicitly benchmarked for domain transfer [7, 11], and early steps toward anomaly reasoning via MLLMs [10]. At the same time, independent scrutiny [9] indicates that current performance, however state-of-the-art relative to prior baselines, likely remains insufficient for direct clinical deployment. Future progress will plausibly depend on tighter integration of the prompt-learning and feature-adaptation paradigms, more standardized multi-category and cross-modality benchmarks, and a deliberate extension of MLLM-based reasoning capabilities — currently demonstrated primarily in general-purpose settings [10] — into the medical domain specifically.
