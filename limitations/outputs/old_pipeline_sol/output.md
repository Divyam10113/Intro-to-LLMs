MVFA’s reported results support parameter-efficient adaptation and cross-dataset transfer on the evaluated medical benchmarks. The limitations below concern the scope and strength of that evidence; they do not imply confirmed data leakage, implementation defects, or author-acknowledged failures.

- **Domain generalization is not fully disentangled from cross-dataset transfer.** Leave-one-dataset-out evaluation does not necessarily establish generalization to unseen modalities or anatomical regions. RESC and OCT17 both contain retinal OCT, and their joint exclusion is not clearly established by the reported protocol. Separately adapted models across folds also do not test one fixed checkpoint against multiple concurrently unseen domains. Grouped holdouts of overlapping dataset families, together with separately controlled and compound shifts in modality, anatomy, disease, institution, scanner, and acquisition protocol, are needed to distinguish these generalization settings.

- **The supervision regime and effective annotation budget require explicit accounting.** Target-zero-shot evaluation can legitimately exclude target-domain supervision while retaining source-domain abnormality labels and available segmentation masks. Nevertheless, a small number of densely annotated images can entail substantial expert effort. The protocol should specify whether \(K\) denotes a total or per-class budget, support-set class composition, normal-reference availability, initialization checkpoints, source annotation quantities, and segmentation-loss handling when masks are unavailable. Comparisons should report annotation effort and access to all reference data, rather than treating image count as a sufficient measure of supervision cost.

- **Sampling independence and the operational definition of normality require validation.** Patient-, volume-, and slide-level independence should be documented for both support and query sampling. Correlated slices or patches can inflate apparent sample efficiency if sampling or uncertainty estimation treats them as independent; this is a methodological risk, not evidence that leakage occurred. A lesion-free BrainMRI slice from a tumor-bearing patient may constitute a valid slice-level negative, but it does not establish patient-level normality or representativeness of healthy controls. Cohort selection and disease–dataset, institution, and acquisition correlations should be audited to distinguish pathology recognition from dataset-specific shortcuts.

- **Binary text alignment does not establish disease-specific semantic grounding.** Averaged normal and abnormal prompts produce two text prototypes, which are compatible with binary prototype supervision without demonstrating detailed medical-language understanding. Prompt ensembling does not establish invariance to meaning-preserving paraphrases or appropriate sensitivity to negation and organ-specific terminology. Classification and segmentation adapters distinguish tasks; their separation does not itself demonstrate anatomical–pathological disentanglement. Disease-held-out evaluation, semantically controlled prompt perturbations, and capacity- and supervision-matched text-free, learned-prototype, and randomized-prototype controls are needed. Tests involving benign variants, devices, postoperative changes, and acquisition artifacts should use explicitly defined clinical abnormality criteria.

- **Native spatial resolution and input granularity constrain localization claims.** The four feature levels correspond to transformer depths rather than progressively finer spatial grids. With nominal \(240\times240\) inputs and \(14\times14\) patches, the native prediction lattice is approximately \(17\times17\), depending on preprocessing. Interpolation increases output-map dimensions without adding independently resolved subpatch evidence, while resizing can suppress small or subtle findings. Slice-level MRI and CT experiments and histopathology-patch evaluation do not establish volumetric consistency, whole-slide aggregation, or patient-level localization. Resolution-dependent performance, physical pixel or voxel spacing, lesion-size sensitivity, and full-examination aggregation require separate evaluation.

- **Normal-reference matching relies on unvalidated coverage and correspondence assumptions.** A small normal-reference set may inadequately represent anatomical position, demographic variation, acquisition conditions, and benign appearances. Anatomically unconstrained nearest-neighbor patch matching can potentially associate abnormal tissue with visually similar normal structures elsewhere or assign high anomaly scores to uncommon healthy appearances. These are plausible failure modes rather than demonstrated errors. Evaluation should quantify sensitivity to reference selection, mislabeled or contaminated normal banks, population and institution diversity, and the dependence of distance-score distributions on reference-bank size and composition.

- **Cross-task consistency, fusion scaling, and score calibration remain unresolved.** Maximum-patch classification pooling concentrates its direct gradient on maximizing patches, although other losses can provide additional training signals. This objective does not ensure that the classification evidence agrees with the separate segmentation stream or is lesion-grounded. For unit-normalized features, a reference score defined as \(1-\cos(\cdot,\cdot)\) lies in \([0,2]\), whereas softmax outputs lie in \([0,1]\). Equal nominal fusion weights therefore do not establish comparable effective contributions or calibrated probabilities. Lesion-consistent aggregation, classification–segmentation agreement, fusion-weight sensitivity, and calibration should be evaluated using only permitted source/support data, without target-test-label tuning.

- **Benchmark discrimination does not establish clinical operating performance.** Image- and pixel-level AUROC measure ranking rather than boundary fidelity, lesion extent, diagnostic performance at deployable thresholds, or clinical workload. Evaluation should include Dice/IoU, boundary accuracy, lesion-wise and size-stratified sensitivity, false positives per examination, sensitivity at prespecified specificity, and precision–recall or predictive values under realistic prevalence. Thresholds intended for deployment should be fixed before target testing. Independent external patient cohorts, subgroup analyses, reader studies, prospective workflow assessment, and validated abstention or referral pathways are needed before clinical utility or safety can be inferred.

- **Statistical uncertainty and comparator fairness are insufficiently characterized.** Without documented repeated support draws, training-seed variation, and clustered confidence intervals, non-monotonic few-shot learning curves cannot distinguish sampling variability from overfitting. Patient-, volume-, or slide-clustered uncertainty estimates and paired comparisons are required. Comparisons with normal-only methods remain informative at the system level, but they do not isolate architectural gains when access to abnormal labels or masks differs. Both supervision-matched and annotation-cost-matched analyses should control backbone/pretraining exposure, resolution, support/reference sets, and tuning budgets. Aggregate results should retain per-dataset heterogeneity, including the reported LiverCT gains and lower zero-shot BrainMRI segmentation performance relative to APRIL-GAN.

- **Ablations do not fully separate architectural mechanisms or incremental utility.** The parameter- and loss-matched adapter-versus-projector comparison provides useful evidence. However, feature injection and cross-level gradient coupling change together, limiting attribution to either mechanism individually. A parameter- and compute-matched single-stream control is also needed to distinguish task separation from additional capacity. The reported four-level ensemble improves over the best individual level by **0.13 percentage points for classification and 0.05 for segmentation**. These point estimates require paired uncertainty analysis and incremental computational profiling before statistically reliable or practically meaningful ensemble benefits can be established.

- **Parameter efficiency does not establish end-to-end efficiency or deployment feasibility.** Freezing ViT-L/14 avoids backbone parameter updates but does not eliminate backbone inference costs or gradient propagation through downstream frozen blocks when early inserted adapters are trained. Assuming \(L\) feature levels, \(K_n\) normal-reference images, \(G\) patches per query and reference image, and feature dimension \(d\), reference storage scales as \(O(LK_nGd)\), while exhaustive patch-to-patch matching requires \(O(LK_nG^2d)\) work per query image. Higher resolution and full-volume or whole-slide coverage increase these costs. Peak memory, training time, per-examination latency, throughput, retrieval compression/indexing, hardware feasibility, and multicenter data-access and privacy requirements remain to be characterized.

**New research problem—budget-constrained, calibrated open-domain medical anomaly detection:** Given heterogeneous, patient-grouped source datasets \(D_s\) with abnormality labels and partially observed lesion masks, and an optional target support set \(S_t\), learn a single source-trained vision–language model \(F_\theta(X,S_t)=(\hat p_{\mathrm{patient}},\hat M_{\mathrm{lesion}},u)\), where \(X\) denotes a complete examination or registered image/slide-tile collection and \(u\) supports an explicit abstention policy. Learned network weights must remain fixed across external domains; target-specific retrieval or calibration may occur only through a predeclared support-conditioning procedure. Require \(c(D_s)\le B_s\) and \(c(S_t)\le B_t\), with cost accounting for annotation effort and access to all reference data; target-zero-shot deployment additionally requires \(S_t=\varnothing\) when \(B_t=0\). The objective is to characterize the achievable robustness–calibration–coverage frontier, for example through \(\min_\theta\sup_{Q\in\mathcal Q}\{R_Q(\theta\mid a_\theta=1)+\eta\,\mathrm{CalErr}_Q(\theta\mid a_\theta=1)\}\), where \(a_\theta=1\) denotes non-abstention and \(R_Q=\mathbb E_Q[\ell_{\mathrm{patient}}+\lambda\ell_{\mathrm{lesion}}\mid a_\theta=1]\). This objective is subject to memory and latency budgets, prespecified false-positive limits, and nontrivial minimum coverage \(\Pr_Q(a_\theta=1)\ge\gamma>0\) for each admissible domain. The family \(\mathcal Q\) must prespecify separate and compound modality, anatomy, disease, institution, scanner, and acquisition-protocol shifts; it does not imply distribution-free validity outside that family. The central technical challenges are anatomy-conditioned pathology representation learning that suppresses acquisition nuisance without removing clinically relevant context; region-grounded language with controlled semantic sensitivity; explicit treatment of missing masks without assigning unannotated regions negative labels; and spatially coherent multiresolution inference that preserves small-lesion evidence. When support is available, retrieval should be contamination-robust and anatomically conditioned, with lesion-consistent patient aggregation. Training, fusion calibration, threshold selection, and abstention-policy fitting must use only admissible source/support information. Evaluation should use one fixed checkpoint on independent external patients, repeated cost-matched support draws and training seeds, clustered confidence intervals, capacity- and supervision-matched semantic controls, patient- and lesion-level operating metrics, subgroup-specific calibration and coverage, and end-to-end hardware profiling. Prospective reader/workflow and privacy-compatible multi-institution studies should subsequently assess clinical utility and residual deployment risk.
